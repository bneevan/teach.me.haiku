Quick Reference Sheet

Haiku Composition & Craft Matrix

Core mechanics, famous benchmarks, word pairings, and formulas for rapid & deep verse.

1. The Core Architecture

  • The 3-Line English Form: Short — Long — Short (typically 10 to 14 syllables).
  • The Golden Rule: 17 Japanese on (morae) are phonetic time units. 17 English syllables pack twice as much information. Never pad with fluff to reach 17!
  • Two Parts, One Breath: A Fragment (1 line) and a Phrase (2 lines), separated by a cut.
  • The Cut (Kireji): Marked in English by a dash (—), colon (:), or strong syntactic break.

2. Classical Masterpieces Decoded

Matsuo Bashō (1686):
furu ike ya (old pond —)
kawazu tobikomu (a frog jumps into)
mizu no oto (water’s sound)
→ Juxtaposition: Timeless stillness vs. momentary kinetic splash.
Yosa Buson:
On the temple bell
resting, asleep:
a butterfly.
→ Scale & Texture: Heavy bronze vs. weightless fragile wing.

3. Word Categories (Kigo & Senses)

Seasonal Anchors (Kigo):

  • Spring cherry blossoms, melting mud, frogs, thaw, seeds
  • Summer thunderhead, cicadas, heat shimmer, fireflies
  • Autumn harvest moon, scarecrow, cricket, fallen leaves
  • Winter bare oak, frosty breath, freezing rain, blizzard

Sensory Registers: Prefer touch, sound, and smell over just sight. "Cold iron", "distant bell", "smell of wet stone".

4. Relational Strategies (Toriawase)

Deep haiku spark a third meaning across the gap between two contrasting images:

  • Scale: Cosmic / Vast vs. Microscopic (e.g. Galaxy vs. Dewdrop).
  • Permanence: Mountain stone vs. Mayfly lifespan.
  • Motion: Rigid stillness vs. sudden flight.
  • Sensory Cross: Deep silence vs. sudden acoustic chime.
  • Rule of Distance: If images are too close, it’s boring. If too far, it’s nonsense. Target the resonant spark.

5. The 60-Second "Snapshot" Formula

Best for: Spontaneous capture, daily journaling, walking walks (ginko).

  1. Line 1 (Anchor): Where or when (e.g., morning frost)
  2. Line 2 (Action/Subject): What is moving or present (e.g., a crow snaps a pine twig)
  3. Line 3 (Sensory Touch): The physical impression (e.g., the sharp echo)
morning frost —
a crow snaps a pine twig,
the sharp echo.

6. The Deep Contemplative Formula

Best for: Emotional resonance, wabi-sabi, mono no aware.

  1. Step 1: Select a poignant human state or memory (e.g., grief, aging, solitude). Do not name it.
  2. Step 2: Find a concrete natural object that shares that rhythm (e.g., rusty nail, dry creek bed, autumn moth).
  3. Step 3: Place the cut (—) between your human anchor and the natural image.
hospital parking lot —
the engine ticking down
into November cold

7. The Five Fatal Traps

  1. Padding for 5-7-5: Adding meaningless filler words ("oh so blue", "upon the tree") just to count syllables.
  2. Naming the Emotion: Writing "I feel sad" or "joyful sunset". Show the wet coat or the spilled tea instead.
  3. Rhyming: Rhyme ties the lines in a sing-song loop, destroying the sharp open cut (yohaku).
  4. Anthropomorphism: Avoid "the trees are weeping" or "happy clouds". Keep nature objective and dignified.
  5. Explaining the Meaning: Never tell the reader how the two images connect. Let the spark leap across the cut in their mind.
