1. The Essence of the Instant

Haiku is not a quaint syllabic exercise about gardens; it is an ancient perceptual technology designed to halt the racing analytical mind and pin down an authentic, fleeting moment of reality.

Western Lyric Poetry

Expresses internal psychology, ego, emotional commentary, and metaphor ("My heart is like a singing bird").

Haiku Poetics (Shasei)

Evaporates the poet's ego. Presents empirical sensory facts directly so the reader experiences the phenomenon firsthand.

Audio focus: Writing for the ear and listening for the acoustic spark across silence.

2. The Core Engine: Juxtaposition (Toriawase)

A haiku almost never describes a single, isolated scene. It works by setting two perceptual planes against each other across a structural cut (kireji).

Image A: The Anchor
Enduring, vast, or context (e.g. old temple bell)
⚡ The Cut
(— / : / pause)
Image B: The Kinetic
Fleeting, minute, or sensory (e.g. sleeping butterfly)

Between these two images lies Yohaku (余白): the pregnant white space. If you explain why the images are related, you discharge the electricity. If you keep them separate, the reader's intuition creates the connection.

Audio focus: The principle of desirable difficulty in imagery — neither too close nor too far.

3. Masterworks Decoded

Notice how masters balance scale, sound, texture, and time across the cut:

Matsuo Bashō • 1686
old pond —
a frog jumps into
the sound of water
Dynamics: Ancient stillness (the pond) ↔ sudden acoustic kinetic event (water splash). Eternity pierced by a solitary sound.
Yosa Buson • Edo Period
On the temple bell
resting, asleep:
a butterfly.
Dynamics: Colossal, heavy, resonant bronze bell (capable of deafening sound) ↔ gossamer, silent, vulnerable butterfly wing.
Kobayashi Issa • 1819
O snail —
climb Mount Fuji,
but slowly, slowly!
Dynamics: Japan's most massive sacred mountain ↔ the tiniest, slowest creature. A tender, existential kinship.
Nick Virgilio • Modern Classic
bass
picking bugs
off the moon
Dynamics: Only seven words. The cosmic moon reflected on the water's surface broken by the mouth of a wild fish.

4. The Syllable Myth & The Senryu Trap

The belief that English haiku must follow a rigid 5-7-5 syllable formula is a historical translation misunderstanding.

Dimension Japanese On (Morae) English Syllables
Acoustic Duration Uniform short phonetic pulse (vowel duration). Variable consonant-heavy word chunks.
Information Density Low. 17 on contains roughly 6 to 8 words. High. 17 English syllables can hold 12 to 16 words.
Modern Recommendation Follow traditional 5-7-5 teikei cadence. Write 3 lines (short-long-short), total 10 to 14 syllables.
Haiku vs. Senryu:
  • Haiku: Grounded in nature, ecology, existential presence, and seasonal awareness (kigo). Tone is reverent, contemplative, or bittersweet (mono no aware).
  • Senryu: Focuses on human relationships, psychological ironies, workplace cynicism, or comedy. No seasonal word required.

5. Composition Heuristics: Fast vs. Deep

The 60-Second "Snapshot"

Use on walks (ginko / haiku walks) or when something in your immediate surroundings catches your eye.

  1. Line 1 (Anchor): Where/When — November drizzle
  2. Line 2 (Action): What moves — a yellow school bus idles
  3. Line 3 (Sensory): The echo — red tail lights in puddles

The Deep Contemplative Cut

Use when dealing with unspoken emotion, solitude, love, or the passage of years.

  1. Rule 1: Identify the human feeling, then delete the word.
  2. Rule 2: Select a modest physical object carrying that texture (rust, stone, dry leaves).
  3. Rule 3: Anchor it to a seasonal fact across a dash.
Common Seasonal Categories (Kigo) & Relational Axes:
Spring: Cherry blossoms, thaw, mud, seeds Summer: Cicadas, thunderhead, dust, shade Autumn: Harvest moon, crickets, smoke, chill Winter: Bare branch, frost, dry snow, silence

1. The Essence of the Instant: Welcome to Mastering Haiku. Most people think a haiku is just a five-seven-five syllable poem about flowers. But in reality, haiku is a three-hundred-year-old technology for capturing the fleeting present moment. When you write a true haiku, you are not describing an abstract idea. You are holding two physical realities side by side, allowing a sudden spark of insight to leap across the gap. By the end of this lesson, you will know how to construct both spontaneous sixty-second snapshots and layered, deeply moving verse that lingers in the mind.

2. The Core Engine: Juxtaposition & The Cut: Let us examine the engine of a haiku. In classical poetics, this is called toriawase, or juxtaposition. A great haiku almost never talks about just one thing. Instead, it places two sensory images together, separated by a structural cut known as a kireji. In English, we mark this cut with a dash, a colon, or a line break. Think of it like an electric circuit. One image is the positive terminal. The other image is the negative terminal. When you place them in the same three lines, the spark leaps across the empty space. That empty space is called yohaku. If you explain the connection to the reader, you kill the poem. Your job is simply to present the two images cleanly, and let the reader feel the spark.

3. Masterwork Analysis: Bashō & Buson: Consider the most famous haiku in world history, composed by Matsuo Basho in sixteen eighty-six. In English: Old pond. A frog jumps in. The sound of water. Notice the structure. The first image is the old pond: ancient, still, unchanging stone and water. Then comes the cut. Then the second image: a tiny living frog, a sudden splash, the fleeting acoustic sound. Basho contrasts eternity with a split-second splash. Now listen to Yosa Buson: On the temple bell, resting, asleep: a butterfly. Look at the sensory pairing: a massive, cold, resonant bronze temple bell, contrasted with the delicate, silent, paper-thin wing of a sleeping butterfly. The poetry is not in the bell. The poetry is not in the butterfly. The poetry lives in the silence between them.

4. The 5-7-5 Syllable Myth & Modern Craft: Here is the biggest misconception in modern poetry: counting five, seven, five syllables in English. In Japanese, poets count on, or morae. These are acoustic time units, much shorter than English syllables. Saying Tokyo takes two English syllables, but four Japanese on. If you cram seventeen English syllables into a poem, it becomes bloated, cluttered with filler words like the, and, or very. Contemporary English masters write in three lines with a short, long, short rhythm, usually between ten and fourteen syllables. Furthermore, beware of senryu. If your poem focuses on human irony, workplace irritation, or humor without nature or a season, it is a senryu, not a haiku. Haiku demands objective presence and seasonal resonance, known as kigo.

5. Technique: Fast Snapshots vs Deep Verse: Now, how do you actually write them? You have two primary techniques. For a quick sixty-second haiku, use the Three-Beat Snapshot. Line one sets the anchor: where or when, such as autumn dusk. Line two captures the immediate movement: a crow snaps a pine twig. Line three delivers the sensory echo: the sharp snap lingering in cold air. To write a deep, thought-provoking haiku, use the Resonant Cut method. Start with a quiet human emotion like longing or aging, but never name it. Instead, find a humble, concrete physical object that mirrors that feeling: a rusty hinge, water dripping from a gutter, a moth against a window screen. Pair that object across a dash with a broader seasonal truth. The emotion will radiate without you ever speaking it.

6. Summary & Daily Practice: To summarize: a great haiku is a three-line sensory encounter. It relies on two juxtaposed images, a sharp cut, and an unsaid resonance. Forget the rigid seventeen syllable rule; focus on ten to fourteen syllables that capture a single breath. Today, step outside or look out your window. Pick one thing that is still, and one thing that is moving. Place a dash between them. You have written a haiku. Review your quick reference sheet in the workspace, and capture your daily snapshot.
