1. The Lens of Shasei: Stop Writing “Poetry”

The single greatest obstacle for poets is the impulse to sound profound. In the late 19th century, master Masaoka Shiki rescued haiku from stale mannerisms with a revolutionary principle borrowed from Western plein-air painting: shasei (写生)—sketching directly from life.

The Editorial Trap (Ego-Led) “I gaze upon the tragic forest, feeling the eternal sorrow of autumn as winter approaches.”
Tells the reader what to feel; clutters the scene with emotional claims and philosophical posturing.
The Shasei Camera (Reality-Led) “bare oak — / cold drizzle clicking / against the telegraph wire”
Records empirical sensory facts. The sadness is not named; it arises organically in the reader’s mind.

Audio focus: Why a detached, honest camera lens has more emotional power than an emotional poet.

2. The Aesthetics of Imperfection: Wabi-Sabi & Mono no Aware

Where Western classicism venerates pristine symmetry and eternal monuments, haiku poetics turns its eye toward the transient, humble, and weathered.

❧ Wabi-Sabi (侘寂)

The beauty of the weathered, imperfect, and modest. A rusted hinge, a warped porch board, a chipped ceramic bowl, peeling green paint on a wooden skiff. It embraces the natural patina of time and wear.

❧ Mono no Aware (物の哀れ)

The pathos of transience. The gentle, wistful awareness that all things pass away. Drifting cherry petals in a gutter, the smoke of burning dry leaves, autumn dusk arriving ten minutes earlier.

The Rule of Restraint: Never describe an object as “old”, “weathered”, or “sad”. Name the physical detail that reveals the wear: flaking rust, sun-bleached cedar, curled edges.

3. Beyond Juxtaposition: The Art of Ichibutsu-jitate

In Chapters I and II, we relied on toriawase: slamming two distinct images across a cut. But what if you are captivated by a solitary subject? Master poets turn to ichibutsu-jitate (一物仕立て)—the “one-thing composition.”

The 3-Beat Internal Transformation Formula:

Instead of colliding two objects, observe a single subject shifting across time, posture, or stillness:

Line 1: Stance / Anchor
The Subject Identified: Ground the physical entity in space (e.g. a dry oak leaf).
Line 2: Micro-Shift
The Physical Motion: Describe its subtle kinetic transition (e.g. spinning in the river eddy).
Line 3: Settling Rest
The New Equilibrium: The motion resolves into stillness (e.g. snags on sunken stone).

Audio focus: How internal movement replaces the need for a second contrasting image.

4. Adjective Poison & The Concrete Verb

Adjectives are the poison of haiku because they rob the reader of their own sensory discovery. Compare these revisions:

Adjective-Bloated (Amateur) Noun & Verb Shasei (Master)
a cold, depressing rain
on the sad abandoned farm
feeling lonely 		november rain —
an empty feed trough fills
with yellow pine needles
beautiful bright morning
cheerful birds singing joyfully
such sweet blossoms 		sun on the sill —
a sparrow shakes dew
from the cherry branch

Notice: In the right-hand column, not a single emotion is declared. The feed trough filling with needles evokes abandonment far more intimately than the word “depressing” ever could.

5. Masterwork Deep Dives: Shiki & Buson

Examine how the two greatest sensory painters in haiku history record empirical moments:

Masaoka Shiki (1895) • Sensory Concreteness
I bite into a persimmon —
a bell tolls,
Hōryū-ji temple.
Analysis: The bitter-sweet orange taste of autumn fruit directly juxtaposed with the low acoustic resonance of an ancient temple bell. Taste meets hearing; the temporal harvest meets eternal stone. Zero poetic embellishment.
Yosa Buson • Pure Ichibutsu-jitate
A single petal
of the cherry blossom fell —
then another, then a petal.
Analysis: No second contrasting image. The entire poem focuses with microscopic stillness on the descending cherry blossoms. The repetition mimics the slow, inexorable departure of spring (mono no aware).

1. The Lens of Shasei: Welcome to Lesson Three. In our previous sessions, you mastered the electric cut, the seasonal code of kigo, and the distance between images. Today, we confront the single biggest trap in all of poetry: the urge to sound poetic. In late nineteenth-century Japan, the master Masaoka Shiki revolutionized haiku with a single radical concept borrowed from Western painting: shasei, which means sketching directly from life. Shiki realized that when a poet tries to be profound, they end up writing clichés. But when a poet acts like a detached, honest camera lens, the world reveals its own quiet power. Today, you will learn to see without editorializing, to discover the sacred beauty in the weathered and broken, and to master the single-object poem.

2. Wabi-Sabi and the Imperfect World: Western literature often worships the monumental, the eternal, and the pristine: marble statues, golden crowns, and triumphant heroes. Japanese poetics, by contrast, turns its gaze toward wabi-sabi and mono no aware. Wabi-sabi is the austere beauty of things that are imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete: a rusted gate hinge, peeling paint on an old barn, a cracked celadon teacup, or a dry thistle rattling in the wind. Mono no aware is the gentle ache of time slipping away: blossom petals washing down a gutter, or the chill in a room after the heater shuts off. When you write haiku, you do not need to invent drama. The patina of age and the mark of time already carry profound emotional weight. Your task is simply to notice them.

3. The Single-Object Poem: Ichibutsu-jitate: So far, we have focused on toriawase: slamming two different images together across a cut to spark a third meaning. But what happens when you are gripped by a single, solitary subject? Classical masters developed ichibutsu-jitate, or the one-thing composition. In an ichibutsu-jitate poem, there is no second image. The entire three lines dwell on a single object or creature. How does this produce a poem instead of a boring label? Through internal transformation. You observe the subject across three micro-moments: its posture, its slight physical shift, and its settling stillness. A single fallen leaf, turning slowly in an eddy of river water, until it snags on a submerged pebble. One subject, fully inhabited.

5. Adjective Poison and the Concrete Verb: Let us talk about the enemy of haiku: adjectives. When an amateur writer wants you to feel sad, they write: a sad old tree in the lonely cold forest. Notice what happened: sad and lonely are emotional instructions. They tell the reader what to feel instead of showing what happened. In haiku, every adjective is suspect. If you remove the words sad and lonely, what remains? Black oak branches clicking against the telegraph wire. The word black is sensory. The verb clicking is acoustic. The wire is concrete. By removing emotional editorializing, you allow the reader to experience the loneliness for themselves. In haiku, nouns and verbs do the work; adjectives only get in the way.

6. Masterworks: Shiki and Buson: Listen to Masaoka Shiki’s most celebrated masterpiece, written in eighteen ninety-five: I bite into a persimmon, and a bell tolls, Hōryū-ji temple. Notice the utter simplicity. He does not tell you he is contemplating ancient Buddhist history or feeling peaceful. He tastes the cold, sweet, orange flesh of an autumn persimmon. At that exact moment, across the misty valley, the deep bronze bell of the ancient temple tolls. The physical taste of autumn meets the ancient spiritual sound. Now listen to Buson: A single petal of the cherry blossom fell; then another; then a petal. This is pure ichibutsu-jitate. No grand philosophy. Just the quiet, relentless mathematics of departure.

8. Summary and the Ginko Walk: To summarize: shasei is sketching empirical reality without poetic decoration. Wabi-sabi teaches us to find beauty in the worn, the humble, and the decaying. And ichibutsu-jitate shows that a single object, observed with fierce stillness, holds an entire universe. Your assignment today is a ginko: a classical haiku walking meditation. Step outside for ten minutes. Do not look for dramatic sunsets. Look for something weathered: a warped fence board, an oil stain on asphalt, a dried seedpod. Write three lines about it. Ban every adjective. Let the thing itself speak.
